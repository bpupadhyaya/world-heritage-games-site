Highland Bagpipe Chanter and Drone

Also known as Piobaireachd · Piping

The Great Highland Bagpipe pairs a nine-note chanter, played with the fingers, with three drone pipes that sound one steady chord from a bag of air. Players add quick grace notes to separate and shape the tune, for marches, dances and slow airs.

How to play

  1. Look at the chanter: dark discs are finger holes you cover, open holes let the note rise. Each pill is one of the nine notes.
  2. Notes travel down the pipe as breath beads. Tap the note's pill as its bead lands in the ring.
  3. Keep to the stride of the march; pipers never stop the sound, so steady, even timing matters more than speed.
  4. Choose Watch and learn to hear and see a whole tune, slow it down, then try it yourself.
Rules at a glance
  • The chanter has nine notes, from Low G up to High A, in a mixolydian-style scale.
  • Each note is struck when its bead reaches the ring: close to the beat scores perfect, a little off scores good, later is a miss.
  • Three tunes are included: a march phrase, a slow air and a figure showing how cuts separate repeated notes.
  • The tune plays twice through; stars depend on the share of the maximum score you reach.
  • Fingerings are drawn as a simple lift-from-the-bottom chart; real chanters use a few cross-fingerings.

About the tradition

Piping grew in the Scottish Highlands and is now played in bands and as a solo art around the world. Classical solo pieces, called piobaireachd, are slow themes with variations; marches, strathspeys and reels are the music for dancing and parades.

Origins

Usually placed in the era of Crossroads and Golden Ages (about 500 to 1500), although its exact origins are uncertain, within the cultural world of Celtic and Atlantic Europe.
