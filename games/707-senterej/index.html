Senterej

Also known as Sant'raj · Shatranj-like · Ethiopian chess

The chess of the Ethiopian highlands, a living descendant of the old shatranj: a short-stepping sew beside the negus, elephants that leap two squares diagonally, and kings that do not face each other across the board.

How to play

  1. Move one piece per turn and capture by landing on an enemy piece; each piece has its own fixed way of moving.
  2. Elephants (fil) leap exactly two squares on a diagonal, horses (ferese) leap like a chess knight, rooks (der) run along rows and columns.
  3. Foot soldiers (medeq) step forward one square, capture diagonally forward and become a sew on the far edge.
  4. Trap the opposing negus so it cannot escape attack to win.
Rules at a glance
  • The back row reads der, ferese, fil, sew, negus, fil, ferese, der from the lower player; the opposing army is turned half a circle, so the two negus stand on different files.
  • The negus steps one square in any direction; the sew steps one square diagonally; the fil leaps two squares diagonally, over anything in between; the ferese leaps like a knight; the der moves any distance along a row or column.
  • Soldiers step one square forward, capture diagonally forward and become a sew when they reach the far row. There is no double step, no capture in passing and no castling.
  • A negus may never be left under attack; trapping it with no escape wins.
  • A player with no legal move who is not under attack draws.
  • The same position three times is a draw.
  • In the village game both sides first set up their armies freely without taking turns until the first capture; in this edition the sides move in turn from the start.

About the tradition

Senterej is played in Ethiopia and Eritrea, in cafes and under trees, and is the last widely played form of the medieval shatranj. Local custom varies on promotion, on how a lone king may be hunted and on the free opening phase, so this edition follows the common core.

Origins

Usually placed in the era of Crossroads and Golden Ages (about 500 to 1500), although its exact origins are uncertain, within the cultural world of Africa: Nile and the Horn.
